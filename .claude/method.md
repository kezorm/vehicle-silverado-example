# The asset-record method — v1.1.0

> **Verbatim copy. Do not edit this file.** It is identical in every record
> created from the asset-record skill, which is what makes it replaceable:
> to update, overwrite it from the current skill and review `../CLAUDE.md` for
> conflicts. Anything specific to *this* thing belongs in `../CLAUDE.md`, not
> here. `md5sum` it against the skill's copy to see whether this record is
> current. Changes between versions are listed in the skill's `CHANGELOG.md`.

This is a record of a thing someone owns — not a software project. Its value
comes from being *current and consistent*. A fact recorded in one file and
contradicted in another is worse than not recording it at all.

An assistant working in this folder reads this automatically, imported from
`../CLAUDE.md`. A person can read it in five minutes. Both should.

---

## Structure emerges; it isn't prescribed

There is no fixed folder tree here on purpose. A vehicle, a house and a camera
kit need genuinely different things, and empty folders guessed at on day one are
worse than no folders — they invite filing documents where they don't belong
because a slot exists.

**Make a folder when a document arrives that needs one.** A car record grows
`service/`, `reference/labels/`, maybe `tires/`. A house grows `permits/`,
`appliances/`, `contractors/`, `insurance/`. Neither should have the other's.

What makes separate records feel like siblings is not their directories. It's
this file, and a README that works the same way.

---

## Two jobs, two clocks

This record does two things, and it's easy to let one eat the other.

**Tell you what needs attention now** — the ranked open items in `README.md`.
Episodic: it matters intensely for a week, then the item closes.

**Answer questions later** — what oil it takes, when the pump was replaced, what
the paint code is, who did the work in 2027 and what exactly they did.
Cumulative, and usually why the record still exists in ten years.

The instinct when filing is to ask *"so what — does this change anything?"* and
drop what doesn't. **That test is wrong for archival facts.** A paint code
changes nothing today and is exactly what you want in 2035 with a scratched
fender. Serial numbers, dimensions, vendor names, the full text of a data plate,
what a previous owner claimed and whether anyone verified it — none of it is
actionable, and all of it is the point.

**Capture completely; triage separately.** Transcribe the whole plate, not the
two fields that matter this week. Record the full service detail, not just the
part that prompted a note. `README.md` holds what's urgent; everything else
holds what's true.

**Write for a reader arriving in ten years knowing nothing** — quite possibly
you, having forgotten. Spell out the abbreviation, say *why* a decision was
made, name the vendor and the town. What's obvious today is exactly what won't
be.

## The core rule

**When new information arrives, propagate it everywhere it belongs — in the same
sitting, before calling it done.**

New information means a document dropped in `_inbox/`, an inspection, an invoice,
a measurement, or something the owner simply says ("I replaced the filter last
week"). Spoken facts are filings too: log them with part numbers and a date.

Filing the artifact is not the task. **Updating what the record concludes from it
is the task.** Three layers, every time:

1. **The artifact** — original preserved intact, sensibly named, with a text
   extraction beside it if it's a PDF
2. **Whatever write-up covers that subject**
3. **`README.md`** — status, open items, upcoming. **The one people forget.**

If it genuinely changes nothing at the top, say so rather than skipping silently.

## Document roles

Roles, not paths — the paths will differ between records.

| Role | Rewritable? |
|---|---|
| **The status document** (`README.md`) — the only description of *current* state | **Yes — always rewrite to stay true** |
| **Reference** — specs, parts, part numbers | Yes, as facts firm up |
| **Logs** — what was done, when | Append; don't rewrite history |
| **Dated analyses** — what was believed *at that time* | **No — annotate, never rewrite** |

When later information changes a past conclusion, add a dated note inside the
original:

> **Added 2027-03-02.** Records filed after this was written show no replacement
> on record in eight years, which supports the hypothesis above.

When a past conclusion turns out to be **wrong**, withdraw it in place rather
than deleting it:

> **Corrected 2027-03-02.** An earlier version said this "was skipped." That was
> unsupported and has been withdrawn. What's known is that no record exists
> after 2020.

Preserving the correction is worth more than a clean-looking document. It tells a
reader the record is maintained by someone who checks.

## Filing

1. `bin/inbox-status` — see what arrived
2. **Read every file before filing it.** Never route on filename alone. The
   filename is a guess; the contents are the fact. Files named `IMG_0854` turn
   out to be data plates carrying part numbers and a previous owner's
   handwritten service note.
3. **Deduplicate by checksum** — people re-send things
4. Put it where its content says it belongs, making the folder if needed
5. **Rename so a human can read it** — `survey-2019-04-11.pdf`, not
   `Document_2019_scan0043.pdf`. Renaming in bulk? Keep a provenance file
   mapping new names to originals.
6. `bin/extract-text` — a `.txt` beside every PDF. **The highest-leverage habit
   here.** It makes the record greppable, and an archived manual then beats the
   internet for questions about *this* item. But see the warning below about
   tables.
7. **Transcribe photographed labels into text.** A photo of a data plate isn't
   data until the numbers are searchable.
8. Write up what it says
9. **Update `README.md`**
10. `bin/check-links`
11. Commit with a message stating what was **learned**, not what moved

```
Add April service; refrigerant leak suspected

Technician notes the system was 1.2 lb low on a 5.8 lb charge — about 20%.
Opened as item 2. Humidifier item closed; the invoice shows it was done.
```

## Evidence standards

Someone spends money against these conclusions.

**Tag confidence.** **[confirmed]** for facts from the item itself or its official
documentation; **[verify]** for the typical value not yet checked against this
specific one. Never state a part number, capacity or interval as fact merely
because it's typical — manufacturers make running changes, and a wrong number
costs a return trip.

**Absence of a record is not evidence of absence.** Write "no record of X since
\<date\>" — never "X was skipped," "was missed," or "was never done." A gap has
two explanations, and nothing in the gap distinguishes them. Not everything is
done by a professional; not everything a professional does gets reported.

This error is tempting because it usually doesn't change the recommendation —
which is also why it's pointless. The honest framing reaches the same action:

> We don't know whether this was done. It degrades invisibly and can't be judged
> by eye. If it was done recently, redoing it costs little. If it wasn't, it's
> years overdue. So do it, and start the clock from a date we actually own.

Same caution for thin data generally: a database showing zero reports for an
uncommon item is weak evidence of absence, not proof of a clean record.

**When sources conflict, record the conflict and name which one governs.** Don't
silently pick — if you choose wrong, nothing later reveals the error.

**Separate urgency from importance.** Inflating a finding spends the credibility
you need when something genuinely is urgent.

**Flag your own weakest claim.** Every research pass produces one conclusion
resting on thinner evidence. Name it.

**Don't trust an extracted table without checking a row.** `pdftotext`
reconstructs columns from character positions and gets tables wrong *silently* —
and records like this one are full of tables that matter: service schedules,
spec charts, parts lists. In one real record it misread a maintenance chart and
produced the wrong replacement interval for a part whose failure destroys the
engine. Nothing about the output looked broken.

Verify one value you can confirm independently before relying on the rest. If a
table spans pages, anchor each row to *its own page's* header — page indentation
shifts more often than you'd expect. And when a document's whole value is in its
tables, re-extract with a structure-aware tool:

```sh
uvx docling --to md service-schedule.pdf
```

Docling (IBM, MIT-licensed) runs a layout model plus a dedicated table model. No
install needed via `uvx`; first run downloads models. It isn't a requirement —
`pdftotext` stays the default because it's instant and always there. Reach for
docling when the tables *are* the content, or when a spot-check fails.
`bin/extract-text` flags files that look tabular so you know which ones deserve
a second look.

**Watch for the calendar limit.** Where something has both a usage interval and a
time limit, work out which binds first. On lightly used things the calendar
usually wins — and nothing on the item warns you.

## Conventions

- Dated folders and files use `YYYY-MM-DD`
- Keep every original **and** its text extraction
- Estimated readings carry a leading `~` (`~12,400`) with one line nearby saying
  what bounds the estimate. An accepted estimate is fine; an estimate presented
  as a measurement is not.
- `_inbox/` contents stay untracked until triaged
- Verify links before committing

**When something tabular gets repetitive** — fuel fill-ups, hours, seasonal
swaps — put the data in a CSV and let a small script compute the summary into
the README. Markdown is for narrative and judgment; a CSV is better at 200 rows.
Reach for this when it actually hurts, not before.

## Self-containment

**Everything needed to work on this record is in this record.** Nothing depends
on an assistant's memory, a subscription, or one machine. Tools may keep their
own notes; nothing is ever allowed to live *only* there. If you learn something
that matters, write it into a file here — that's the only place it counts.

---

## Before sharing

**Going public is one-way.** Clones, forks and mirrors aren't recalled by
flipping visibility back. Private-first is the reversible order; to share with
specific people, add collaborators — that isn't publication.

Audit for the owner's own information (prices, identifiers, addresses — and
check commit metadata, which carries name and email), third-party copyrighted
material being republished, and anything about other people.

If a public version is genuinely wanted, make it a **separate repository with
fresh history** carrying the method and none of the documents.
